Overview

This homework took us from interpolating a few points to changing the connectivity of a whole triangle mesh. We implemented de Casteljau evaluation for Bézier curves and patches, area-weighted vertex normals, edge flips, edge splits, and Loop subdivision. The most useful lesson was how much bookkeeping lives underneath a simple-looking edit: changing one diagonal means keeping halfedges, twins, faces, vertices, and representative pointers consistent.

Section I: Bezier Curves and Surfaces

Part 1: Bezier curves with 1D de Casteljau subdivision

For each adjacent pair of control points, we calculate (1 − t)Pᵢ + tPᵢ₊₁. That makes a shorter row of points. Our evaluateStep function returns just this next row; repeating the step eventually leaves one point on the curve. The viewer lets us step through each row with E; the green trace shows the full curve when C is on.

We made a six-point curve with a rise, a dip, and another rise so the intermediate levels are easy to follow. We saved it as bzc/six_points.bzc. The first sequence evaluates it at t = 0.37; each image adds one level, ending at the evaluated point.

Part 2: Bezier surfaces with separable 1D de Casteljau

For a surface, we apply the same interpolation to 3D points in two passes. The evaluate1D helper calls evaluateStep until one point remains. We use it to evaluate each row of control points at u, which gives a new column of points, then evaluate that column at v. This separable approach returns one position on the patch for each (u, v). The teapot below comes from bez/teapot.bez.

Section II: Triangle Meshes and Half-Edge Data Structure

Part 3: Area-weighted vertex normals

At each vertex, we walk the incident faces through the halfedge ring using h = h->twin()->next(), skipping virtual boundary faces. For each triangle, the cross product of its two outgoing side vectors points along its normal and has magnitude twice its area. We sum these unnormalized vectors, so larger triangles contribute more, then normalize once at the end. The common factor of two cancels during normalization. The result is the vertex normal used for smooth shading.

Part 4: Edge flip

An interior edge belongs to two triangles, which together form a quadrilateral. A flip replaces their shared diagonal with the other diagonal. We gather all six local halfedges and four vertices first, then reassign the six next, twin, vertex, edge, and face relationships. We also update the face and vertex representative halfedges. The operation reuses the same elements; it does not need new mesh objects.

We checked each face cycle and representative halfedge after repeated flips. A vertex's representative must still begin at that vertex, and each twin pair must describe the same edge in opposite directions. Boundary edges remain unchanged because they do not have a second real triangle to flip into.

Part 5: Edge split

A split inserts a vertex at the selected edge's midpoint. The two adjacent triangles become four. We keep the original edge for one half of the old diagonal, create three more edges and two more faces, then wire the six new halfedges into the four triangle cycles. We mark the midpoint vertex as new. For subdivision, both halves of the original edge keep isNew = false, while the two edges joining the midpoint to the opposite vertices get isNew = true. This keeps the later flip pass from flipping either half of an original edge.

The cube face makes the change easy to see: one diagonal becomes a midpoint connected to the other two corners. We then flipped a neighboring edge across a cube crease to check that the operations work together. Choosing that edge avoids the collinear triangle that can result from immediately flipping a spoke in a flat, newly split face. Because the neighboring triangles lie on different planes, that flip changes the surface shape as well as its connectivity. Our checks verify face cycles, twin pairs, representative pointers, and the expected element counts after each edit.

We also implemented boundary splits for extra credit. We split only the real triangle into two, adding one vertex, two edges, one face, and four halfedges. On the virtual boundary face, the walk B → A becomes B → M → A; the loop stays closed. The midpoint's representative still lies on a segment of the original edge. We handle either orientation of the original edge's representative.

Part 6: Loop subdivision for mesh upsampling

We first record the original edges and compute all new positions from the untouched mesh. For an old vertex of degree n, each neighbor gets weight u, where u = 3/16 when n = 3 and otherwise u = 3/(8n). The old vertex keeps weight 1 − nu. A new edge point gets 3/8 from each endpoint and 1/8 from each of the two opposite vertices.

Only after saving those positions do we split every original edge. Then we flip new edges that connect one old and one new vertex, and finally assign the saved positions. Keeping the geometry update until the end matters: otherwise an early move would affect later weights.

Each iteration smooths sharp edges and corners because old vertices move toward their neighbors and the new edge points average nearby positions. The original cube has an uneven triangulation: each face diagonal gives its endpoints an extra neighbor. Corners therefore have different degrees and neighbor directions, so repeated averaging makes the result asymmetric.

Flipping one face diagonal changes which corners receive that influence but does not remove the imbalance. To make the cube subdivide symmetrically, we instead split all six face diagonals. Each face then has a center connected to its four corners. All eight original corners have degree six with equivalent neighborhoods, and all six face centers have degree four. Loop treats corresponding vertices the same, so the symmetry survives repeated subdivision.

We separately tested pre-splitting the cube's twelve physical edges, where two faces meet at a right angle. This puts a midpoint closer to each corner along those edges. The comparison below uses two Loop iterations in both cases. The added samples reduce the rounding and shrinkage in this example, but do not create a permanent crease: repeated subdivision still smooths the feature.

Extra Credit

Boundary splits and boundary-aware Loop subdivision

For an edge on the boundary, the new vertex stays at its midpoint rather than using two opposite vertices. An old boundary vertex moves to 3/4 P + 1/8 N₁ + 1/8 N₂, where N₁ and N₂ are its two neighbors along the boundary. Interior vertices keep the usual Loop rule. We calculate every position before splitting edges, and boundary edges are never flipped.

Our open patch has a real interior vertex and one outer boundary loop. The screenshots show a boundary split and two subdivision rounds. We also checked an annulus with two separate boundary loops. Those tests exposed a starter-code omission: a virtual boundary face did not have a representative halfedge. We set that pointer when constructing the loop and keep it valid after a split.

An additional scheme: √3 subdivision

We implemented Kobbelt's √3 subdivision for closed triangle meshes. Press U to use it; L still runs Loop. We insert a centroid into each original face, then replace each original edge with a connection between the centroids on its two sides. Each face becomes three triangles, so the face count grows by three instead of Loop's factor of four.

For an original vertex of degree n, we use α = (4 − 2 cos(2π/n))/9 and update it to (1 − α)P + (α/n)ΣN. Centroids use the original face positions. We build the resulting triangles from saved vertex and face indices, which expresses the centroid split and original-edge flip without introducing another local pointer-rewiring routine. This rebuild invalidates old selections, so the viewer clears them afterward. This scheme supports closed meshes; open meshes use our boundary-aware Loop implementation.

Section III: Creative Mesh - San's Mask

We modeled San's mask from Princess Mononoke, using Megan Cupp's front-view illustration as our visual reference. We focused on the rounded red face, three dark circular openings with ochre rims, cream markings, and pointed ears. The goal was a recognizable mask built from simple shapes.

The face is a shallow ellipsoid. We added three torus-like rings around dark discs, two triangular prisms for the ears, and four tapered cones for the teeth. A larger flattened ellipsoid behind the face suggests the fur outline. Each primitive is a closed component with consistent outward winding; the components overlap at their attachment points. The control mesh has 554 vertices and 1,064 triangles and is saved as artcompetition.dae.

We used basic ambient and Lambert diffuse shading: color × (0.30 + 0.70 max(N · L, 0)). Simple position tests assign red, cream, ochre, and dark colors. Two curves in the face's local coordinates define the painted brow and cheek bands. The cream backing is kept separate from those paint tests using its depth. In MeshEdit, press W and 2 for this shader, and Q to compare flat and smooth normals.

The result is a simplified study. The dark discs suggest eye and mouth openings but are not actual through-holes, and the cream backing has no fur strands. Subdivision rounds the ears and teeth more than in the reference. Even with those limitations, the silhouette, circular features, and color layout make the intended mask readable. We checked the primitive winding, closed edge connectivity, vertex normals, and repeated subdivision before capturing the result.
