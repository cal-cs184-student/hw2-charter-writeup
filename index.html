Overview

This homework took me from interpolating a few points to changing the connectivity of a whole triangle mesh. I implemented de Casteljau evaluation for Bézier curves and patches, area-weighted vertex normals, edge flips, edge splits, and Loop subdivision. The most useful lesson was how much bookkeeping lives underneath a simple-looking edit: changing one diagonal means keeping halfedges, twins, faces, vertices, and representative pointers consistent.

Section I: Bezier Curves and Surfaces

Part 1: Bezier curves with 1D de Casteljau subdivision

For each adjacent pair of control points, I calculate (1 − t)Pᵢ + tPᵢ₊₁. That makes a shorter row of points. Repeating this step eventually leaves one point on the curve. The viewer lets me step through each row with E; the green trace shows the full curve when C is on.

I made a six-point curve with a rise, a dip, and another rise so the intermediate levels are easy to follow. Each image below adds one level, ending at the evaluated point.

Part 2: Bezier surfaces with separable 1D de Casteljau

The 2D version uses the same routine twice. I evaluate each row of 3D control points at u, which gives a new column of points, then evaluate that column at v. This separable approach returns one position on the patch for each (u, v). The teapot below comes from bez/teapot.bez.

Section II: Triangle Meshes and Half-Edge Data Structure

Part 3: Area-weighted vertex normals

At each vertex, I walk the incident faces through the halfedge ring. For each triangle, the cross product of its two outgoing side vectors gives a face normal whose length also reflects its area. I sum those vectors and normalize once at the end. The result is the vertex normal used for smooth shading.

Part 4: Edge flip

An interior edge belongs to two triangles, which together form a quadrilateral. A flip replaces their shared diagonal with the other diagonal. I gather all six local halfedges and four vertices first, then reassign the six next, twin, vertex, edge, and face relationships. I also update the face and vertex representative halfedges. The operation reuses the same elements; it does not need new mesh objects.

Keeping a small before-and-after picture of the two face cycles was the useful debugging trick here. It made it easier to check the winding and to see whether a representative halfedge still began at its vertex. I leave boundary edges alone.

Part 5: Edge split

A split inserts a vertex at the selected edge's midpoint. The two adjacent triangles become four. I keep the original edge for one half of the old diagonal, create three more edges and two more faces, then wire the six new halfedges into the four triangle cycles. I mark the edges and vertex that were created so subdivision can tell old topology from new topology later.

The cube face makes the change easy to see: one diagonal becomes a midpoint connected to the other two corners. I also flipped one of the new edges to check that the two operations work together. As with the flip, writing down each local triangle's vertex order was the clearest way to catch miswired next pointers.

Boundary-edge splits are outside this implementation, so the split routine leaves those edges unchanged.

Part 6: Loop subdivision for mesh upsampling

I first record the original edges and compute all new positions from the untouched mesh. For an old vertex of degree n, each neighbor gets weight u, where u = 3/16 when n = 3 and otherwise u = 3/(8n). The old vertex keeps weight 1 − nu. A new edge point gets 3/8 from each endpoint and 1/8 from each of the two opposite vertices.

Only after saving those positions do I split every original edge. Then I flip new edges that connect one old and one new vertex, and finally assign the saved positions. Keeping the geometry update until the end matters: otherwise an early move would affect later weights.

Each round smooths away sharp edges and corners because the old vertices move toward their neighbors while new vertices are averaged into each triangle. Extra splits near a feature can give it more local support and slow that rounding. The cube also starts with an uneven triangulation: a face diagonal gives its two endpoints different neighborhoods than the other two corners. Repeated averaging carries that difference forward, producing a slightly asymmetric result.
